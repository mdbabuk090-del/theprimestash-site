The Restaurant Rule That Tells You Everything

An old piece of hiring advice — watch how they treat the waiter — turns out to be better supported than the people repeating it realise.

Priya Raman · Aug 22, 2026

The advice is old enough to be a cliché. Take someone to dinner. Watch how they treat the person serving them.

It is repeated so often that most people have stopped asking whether it is actually true. It is worth asking, because the answer is more interesting than the cliché.

Why the test works at all

The usefulness is not that rudeness reveals a bad character. Plenty of decent people are short-tempered when tired.

It is that a restaurant creates a very specific situation: a person who is obliged to be pleasant to you, whom you will never see again, in front of someone whose opinion you care about.

That combination is rare in ordinary life. It isolates a single question — how does this person behave toward someone who cannot answer back and does not matter to their future?

Almost nothing else in a first evening tests that.

The thing to watch is not anger

Overt rudeness is easy and, honestly, uncommon. People on a first evening are performing.

The more revealing signals are quieter.

Does the person acknowledge that the server exists — eye contact, a response to a question — or do they continue talking as though a piece of furniture has approached the table?

When something goes wrong, where does the complaint go? Some people address the problem. Others address the person, in a tone calibrated to be heard by the room.

And afterwards: is it mentioned? A person who was mildly unpleasant and knows it will often make a small joke about it, which is a form of accountability. A person who has not registered it at all has told you the more important thing.

The reverse test

There is a second version, less discussed and arguably more useful. Watch how they behave toward someone with obvious status — a maître d', a senior colleague who happens to be in the room.

The pattern worth noticing is not deference. It is change. A person whose manner shifts sharply upward for the important and sharply downward for the invisible is running a hierarchy, and you are being placed somewhere in it. You just do not know where yet.

Consistency across that gap is the actual signal.

Where the cliché overreaches

It does not tell you whether someone is kind. It tells you whether they perform kindness when there is no incentive to.

Those are different, and one evening cannot distinguish them. Some people are warm to servers and cold to the person they live with, because staff cost nothing and intimacy costs something.

So treat it as a filter, not a verdict. It is very good at ruling people out. It is unreliable at ruling them in.

Why it survives

Because it is one of the few tests that cannot easily be studied for.

Someone can prepare answers about their childhood, their ambitions, their last relationship. Almost nobody thinks to prepare a performance for the person refilling the water — and that is precisely why the moment they do it, you learn something they did not intend to tell you.
